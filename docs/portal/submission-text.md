## Public summary

HappyGut is an AI-native chronic care companion for people living with Crohn's disease and ulcerative colitis. It removes the daily anxiety of eating by answering one question in seconds: "Is this meal likely to trigger me?" A patient photographs a plate of food, a packaged-food label or a menu, or simply speaks in Bangla, English or Banglish, and HappyGut returns a personal, explained verdict with the evidence behind it and a gut-friendly alternative.

What makes HappyGut different is that it does not stop at generic "IBD diet" rules. Food triggers vary widely from person to person, so HappyGut learns what each patient's gut actually tolerates. At its core is the Personal Gut Graph: a clinical knowledge graph that links foods, including everyday Bangladeshi dishes, to their ingredients, their gut-relevant components (such as fat, lactose, insoluble fibre, fermentable carbohydrates and spice) and to published IBD dietary guidance from bodies such as ESPEN, IOIBD and the AGA. Every patient then grows their own layer on top of this graph. As they log meals and complete a 30-second daily check-in, a personal trigger-discovery model estimates, for each food component, how likely it is to be followed by symptoms for that specific person.

Every verdict is graded rather than a binary "safe" label: Likely OK for you, Caution, Likely trigger for you, or Not sure. Each one shows why, citing three kinds of evidence: clinical guidance, what is in the food, and the patient's own history. Language models handle perception (reading photos, labels and Bangla speech) and plain-language explanation, while the verdict itself is computed by a transparent, testable scoring engine. HappyGut asks rather than guesses when it is uncertain, never advises changing medication, and immediately directs red-flag symptoms to a doctor.

Beyond meals, HappyGut runs the patient's care routine: medication and test reminders, weight and symptom tracking with validated clinical indices, an early-warning score that flags a building flare, a generative dietitian that plans weekly meals from foods the patient tolerates, and a one-page report the patient can share with their gastroenterologist before a visit.

Built Bangla-first for low-bandwidth mobile use, and for people who prefer speaking to typing, HappyGut starts in Bangladesh, where IBD is rising but specialist care and localized dietary guidance are limited, and scales by adding cuisines and languages as modules. Privacy is designed in: identifying details are removed by a locally hosted model before any cloud AI call, and patients control what is shared. Population learning is designed around federated learning, which shares model updates rather than personal records, alongside consented, anonymized aggregate statistics that can help researchers understand real-world IBD triggers in under-studied populations.

HappyGut turns everyday meals and check-ins into personal, explainable care: fewer surprise flares, less fear at the table, and better conversations with doctors. For clinics and hospitals, the same data becomes a remote-monitoring view of what happens between appointments.

## Problem statement (What problem does it solve?)

Inflammatory Bowel Disease (IBD), which includes Crohn's disease and ulcerative colitis, is a lifelong condition with no cure that affects millions of people worldwide. It is no longer a mainly Western disease: new cases are rising fastest in newly industrialized regions, including South Asia. Living with IBD means managing unpredictable flare-ups of pain, diarrhoea, bleeding and fatigue that can take away days or weeks of study, work and family life.

1. Every meal is a gamble. Many patients find that certain foods worsen their symptoms, but those triggers differ from person to person. Generic advice such as "avoid spicy food" or "try low-FODMAP" is too broad to follow with confidence. In Bangladesh the problem is sharper: most meals are home-cooked or restaurant dishes with no ingredient label, so at a wedding, an iftar or a family dinner a patient has to guess what is in a biryani, a bhorta or a curry, and how it will affect them.

2. Triggers are discovered too late. Symptoms can appear hours or even a day or two after eating, mixed in with stress, poor sleep and missed doses, so patients rarely connect cause and effect. Many only suspect a food after a painful flare, and some cut out so many foods out of fear that they risk malnutrition, which is already common in IBD.

3. The care routine is exhausting. IBD needs daily medication, regular blood and stool tests, periodic colonoscopies and constant attention to weight and symptoms. Patients juggle this with scattered alarms, notes and memory, and poor medication adherence is linked to a higher risk of relapse.

4. Doctors only see a snapshot. In short, infrequent appointments, gastroenterologists rely on what patients remember. What happened between visits, and the early signs of a building flare, are mostly lost.

5. Real-world knowledge is wasted. The daily experience of millions of patients, especially those eating South Asian diets, is barely represented in research. It sits in personal notebooks and isolated apps, and sharing it raises serious privacy concerns.

The result is fear at every meal, preventable flares and hospital visits, and a gap between patients' daily lives and the clinical care and research meant to help them.

## Solution description (How does your solution work?)

HappyGut is a closed-loop, AI-native care system. Language models handle perception and explanation, a knowledge graph and retrieval pipeline supply clinical evidence, statistical models learn from each individual patient, and a transparent scoring engine makes the final call. It works in five connected parts.

1. Instant Meal Check (multimodal AI + Graph RAG). The patient photographs a plate, a label or a menu, or speaks or types in Bangla, English or Banglish. A vision-language agent identifies the dishes, reads label text and resolves each item against the HappyGut Food Knowledge Graph, which maps Bangladeshi and international dishes to their ingredients, typical preparation and gut-relevant components (fat, lactose, fermentable carbohydrates, insoluble fibre, spice, alcohol, caffeine). A hybrid retrieval pipeline (contextual vector search, keyword search and re-ranking, combined with graph traversal) pulls the relevant IBD dietary guidance from ESPEN, IOIBD and AGA publications. A transparent scoring engine then combines three kinds of evidence (clinical guidance, food composition and the patient's own history) with their current state, such as remission, flare or stricturing Crohn's. The patient gets a graded verdict (Likely OK for you, Caution, Likely trigger for you, or Not sure), a plain-language explanation with citations, a confidence level and a better alternative. If a photo is unclear, HappyGut asks a question instead of guessing.

2. Personal Trigger Discovery (the Personal Gut Graph). Every checked meal is logged automatically. A 30-second daily check-in records symptoms using the validated Harvey-Bradshaw Index for Crohn's or the SCCAI for ulcerative colitis, plus weight, stress, sleep and doses taken. A Bayesian personal model compares symptoms in the 6 to 48 hours after each exposure with the patient's own baseline, adjusts for confounders such as stress and missed doses, and starts from population priors, so it becomes useful within weeks rather than years. Results are written back to the patient's layer of the knowledge graph as learned tolerances, for example "high-fat fried meals: likely trigger, 4 of 5 exposures", which sharpens every future check. We validate this engine on a clearly labelled simulated patient cohort with known, injected triggers, measuring how accurately and how quickly it recovers them.

3. Flare Early Warning. An explainable risk score tracks symptom trends, clinical index changes, medication adherence, weight loss and optional wearable signals (resting heart rate, heart-rate variability, sleep). When risk rises, HappyGut shows which factors drove it, suggests gentle steps such as switching to a gentler meal plan, and recommends contacting the care team. Red-flag symptoms such as heavy bleeding, fever with abdominal pain or signs of bowel obstruction bypass all diet advice and trigger an immediate prompt to seek medical care.

4. Generative Dietitian and Care Manager. A meal-planning agent builds weekly plans and recipes, including familiar Bangladeshi dishes, using only foods the patient tolerates and adapted to flare or remission. Every generated plan is re-checked by the same scoring engine before it is shown. Automated background workflows handle medication reminders, blood test, stool test and colonoscopy schedules, and alerts when weight loss crosses a clinical threshold. With one tap, the patient can share a one-page pre-visit report with their gastroenterologist covering symptom trend, adherence, discovered triggers and early-warning history. HappyGut never recommends starting, stopping or changing medication.

5. Privacy and Research (data sovereignty by design). Identifying details are stripped by a locally hosted open-weight model before any text reaches a cloud AI service. Health records are encrypted, protected by row-level access control and owned by the patient, who decides what to share. For population learning, HappyGut is designed for federated learning: shared trigger and early-warning models improve from model updates, never from raw personal records, protected by secure aggregation and differential privacy. Separately, patients can opt in to contributing anonymized aggregate statistics, such as how often a food is followed by symptoms across a region, to a research data store, giving researchers real-world insight into IBD in under-studied populations without exposing anyone's identity.

The whole experience is Bangla-first, voice-enabled and designed for low-bandwidth Android phones. Cuisines and languages plug in as modules, so HappyGut can scale from Bangladesh to South Asia and the global IBD community. The preliminary prototype implements the Instant Meal Check, Personal Trigger Discovery, the early-warning score and the safety and privacy layers; meal planning, wearables and federated learning are designed and on the roadmap.
