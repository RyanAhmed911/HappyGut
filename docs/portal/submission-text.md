## Public summary

HappyGut is an AI-native chronic care companion for people living with Crohn's disease and ulcerative colitis. It takes the daily uncertainty out of eating by answering one question in seconds: "Is this meal likely to trigger me?" A patient photographs a plate, a packaged-food label or a menu, or types the meal in Bangla or English, and HappyGut returns a graded, explained verdict for each dish, with the evidence behind it and, when one exists, a gentler alternative.

What makes HappyGut different is that it does not stop at generic "IBD diet" rules. Food triggers vary widely from person to person, so HappyGut learns from each patient's own records. Its food knowledge maps 61 everyday Bangladeshi dishes to their gut-relevant components (fat, lactose, fermentable carbohydrates, insoluble fibre, spice and more) and links them to 25 Rule Cards. Each Rule Card cites a published IBD paper confirmed by DOI or PMID and quotes the exact sentence it relies on; the knowledge was reviewed by an LLM judge against those sources, not by clinicians. A two-minute nightly diary records the day's foods, food reactions, stool changes, sleep and stress. The Flare Pattern Finder then shows which foods and habits are linked to that person's worse days, drawn as a Personal Gut Graph, and the app keeps remission and flare periods apart.

Every verdict is graded rather than a binary "safe" label: Likely OK for you, Caution, Likely trigger for you, or Not sure. Each one explains why, using three kinds of evidence: published guidance, what is in the food, and the patient's own diary. A vision-language model identifies the dishes and a language model writes the explanation, but the verdict itself is computed by a transparent, unit-tested scoring engine, so the same meal always gets the same answer. If the AI service is unavailable, typed meals still work and the explanation falls back to a cited template. When a dish is unclear, HappyGut asks rather than guesses. It never advises starting, stopping or changing medication, and red-flag symptoms stop all food advice and direct the patient to a doctor.

Beyond meals, HappyGut includes a transparent flare-risk outlook that shows the reason behind every point, a personal food guide with the patient's verdict for every dish, a week of meal ideas built only from dishes currently rated Likely OK, a nightly diary reminder, and a printable one-page report to take to the gastroenterologist.

Built Bangla-first and mobile-first as an installable web app, HappyGut starts in Bangladesh, where specialist care and localized dietary guidance are limited, and every screen works in Bangla and English. Privacy is designed in: before any text reaches a cloud AI service, our own backend removes phone numbers, ID numbers, email addresses, links and self-introduced names with Bangladesh-specific rules, and adds Microsoft Presidio; photos are stripped of location data. The judges' test accounts hold synthetic sample records, because no real patient data may be used. A public "How it works" page explains the flow, the safeguards, the data sources and the measured results.

HappyGut turns everyday meals and check-ins into personal, explainable insight: fewer guesses at the table, an early warning when flare risk rises, and better-prepared conversations with doctors. Looking ahead, new cuisines and languages plug in as data rather than code, and, with patients' explicit consent, anonymised patterns from many diaries could give researchers the South Asian IBD data that is missing today. A clinic monitoring view, federated learning and research data sharing are designed but not yet built.

## Problem statement (What problem does it solve?)

Inflammatory Bowel Disease (IBD), which includes Crohn's disease and ulcerative colitis, is a lifelong condition with no cure and no known cause that affects millions of people worldwide. It is no longer a mainly Western disease: new cases are rising fastest in newly industrialized regions, including South Asia. Living with IBD means managing unpredictable flare-ups of pain, diarrhoea, bleeding and fatigue that can take away days or weeks of study, work and family life.

1. Every meal is a gamble. Many patients find that certain foods worsen their symptoms, but those triggers differ from person to person. Generic advice such as "avoid spicy food" or "try low-FODMAP" is too broad to follow with confidence. In Bangladesh the problem is sharper: most meals are home-cooked or restaurant dishes with no ingredient label, so at a wedding, an iftar or a family dinner a patient has to guess what is in a biryani, a bhorta or a curry, and how it will affect them.

2. Triggers are discovered too late. Symptoms can appear hours or even a day or two after eating, mixed in with stress, poor sleep and missed doses, so patients rarely connect cause and effect. Many only suspect a food after a painful flare, and some cut out so many foods out of fear that they risk malnutrition, which is already common in IBD.

3. Diagnosis comes late and awareness is low. IBD is "rising at an alarming rate in south Asia", yet data from the region is scarce, especially outside India (Shenoy et al., Lancet Gastroenterol Hepatol 2025). Across 38 centres in 15 countries of South Asia, South-East Asia and the Middle East, Crohn's diagnosis took a median of 12 months and a fifth of Crohn's patients received tuberculosis treatment (Banerjee et al., J Gastroenterol Hepatol 2022). Few people in Bangladesh know the disease.

4. Doctors only see a snapshot. In short, infrequent appointments, gastroenterologists rely on what patients remember. What happened between visits, and the early signs of a building flare, are mostly lost.

5. Real-world knowledge is wasted. The daily experience of millions of patients, especially those eating South Asian diets, is barely represented in research. It sits in personal notebooks and isolated apps, and sharing it raises serious privacy concerns.

The result is fear at every meal, flares that might have been seen coming, and a gap between patients' daily lives and the clinical care and research meant to help them.

## Solution description (How does your solution work?)

HappyGut is an AI-native meal and symptom companion. Language models handle perception and explanation, a food knowledge graph with cited Rule Cards supplies the evidence, a statistical model learns from each patient's own diary, and a transparent scoring engine makes the final call. The prototype works in five connected parts.

1. Instant Meal Check (multimodal AI + knowledge graph). The patient photographs a plate, a packaged-food label or a menu, or types the dishes in Bangla or English. A vision-language model (Gemini) identifies the dishes and reads label text, and each one is resolved against HappyGut's food knowledge graph of 61 Bangladeshi dishes, which understands Bangla names and common English-letter spellings such as "kacchi". The graph maps each dish to its typical ingredients and gut-relevant components and links those components to the Rule Cards that apply to this patient's diagnosis and current state, such as remission, flare or stricturing Crohn's; hard safety rules for strictures apply before any AI output. The verdict engine combines published guidance, food composition and the patient's own diary evidence. The patient gets one of four graded verdicts per dish (Likely OK for you, Caution, Likely trigger for you, or Not sure), the reasons with their citations, a confidence level and, when one exists, a gentler alternative. A language model (Gemini, with a Groq-hosted fallback) writes the plain-language explanation, and an output guard rejects any explanation that disagrees with the engine and falls back to a cited template. When a dish is unclear, HappyGut asks rather than guesses.

2. Nightly Diary and Flare Pattern Finder (the Personal Gut Graph). Every checked meal is logged automatically. Each night, a two-minute diary asks what the patient ate during the day (meals checked earlier are already filled in), whether any food bothered them, and about stool frequency and Bristol type, blood, mucus, urgency, pain, sleep, stress, doses taken or missed, and stressful periods such as exam weeks. From these answers HappyGut calculates the Harvey-Bradshaw Index for Crohn's or the SCCAI for ulcerative colitis. The Flare Pattern Finder, a Bayesian model built only from that patient's diary, compares how often a worse day follows each factor (food components, late nights, short or poor sleep, high stress, exam periods and missed doses) with how often it follows days without that factor. It needs at least three occurrences, reports likely contributors with their counts and uncertainty, flags factors that occur together, and never calls a pattern a proven cause. Food patterns with enough evidence feed back into the patient's next meal checks, so a verdict can say that their own diary links high-fat meals to their worse days. Remission and flare periods are detected from the daily score, confirmed or rejected by the patient, and shown separately on a timeline with their own symptom summaries.

3. Flare Early Warning. A transparent, rule-based outlook scores the next three days from the recent symptom score, sleep, missed doses, upcoming stressful periods such as exams, and recent exposure to the patient's own likely contributors, and shows the points behind every factor. When risk rises, HappyGut suggests general steps such as gentler meals and regular sleep and advises contacting a doctor if symptoms keep rising. Red-flag symptoms such as heavy bleeding, fever with abdominal pain or signs of bowel obstruction stop all food advice and prompt the patient to seek medical care.

4. Food Guide and Pre-visit Report. The food guide shows every known dish with this patient's current verdict and updates immediately when the health profile changes, for example a stricture, lactose intolerance or IBS-like symptoms. Meal ideas build a week from dishes the engine currently rates Likely OK for that person; they are ideas, not a prescription. The patient can print a one-page pre-visit report with symptom trends, flare history, dose logging and their likely contributors, so the gastroenterologist sees what happened between visits. HappyGut never recommends starting, stopping or changing medication.

5. Privacy and safeguards. Before any text reaches a cloud AI service, our own backend removes phone numbers, national ID and birth-registration numbers, email addresses, links and names after self-introductions ("my name is…", "আমার নাম…") using Bangladesh-specific rules, and adds Microsoft Presidio. Photos are stripped of location data before they leave the backend. A red-flag check runs before anything else, in Bangla, English and Bangla typed in English letters. The judges' test accounts hold synthetic sample records; no real patient data is used anywhere.

Measured results. We evaluated the deterministic parts of HappyGut with a reproducible run that uses no language model. On 200 simulated patients, each with 120 days of diary data and known hidden contributors, the Flare Pattern Finder found 293 of 409 (72%). Another 258 reports named habits that occur together with a true contributor in the simulator, such as short sleep alongside late nights; the app marks these "often happens together with". It made 52 false reports. Precision is 85% if those companion reports are set aside and 49% if they are counted as errors. On a development suite written by the team, the red-flag check caught 29 of 29 emergency phrases in English, Bangla and Bangla typed in English letters, and wrongly flagged 0 of 30 ordinary messages. Across 427 verdicts (61 dishes × 7 patient profiles), every verdict was identical when computed twice, every Caution or Likely trigger verdict cited a Rule Card, and all 11 dishes with fibre, skins, seeds or nuts were rated Likely trigger for a person with a stricture. These are simulated-cohort and development-test results, not evidence from real patients or a clinical evaluation. Photo-recognition accuracy, agreement with dietitian ratings and end-to-end response time have not been measured yet.

The app is Bangla-first, also supports English, is mobile-first and installs as a web app. Designed but not yet built: voice input, weight and wearable tracking, medication and test reminders, AI-written meal plans and recipes, retrieval over a full guideline corpus, a clinic monitoring view, and federated learning with consented research data. The preliminary prototype implements the Instant Meal Check, the Nightly Diary with sleep and stress tracking, the Flare Pattern Finder and Personal Gut Graph, the remission and flare timeline, the flare-risk outlook, the food guide and meal ideas, the pre-visit report, and the safety and privacy layers.
